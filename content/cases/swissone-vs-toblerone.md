### 4. The Swiss Chocolate Industry

#### History
- Francois-Louis Cailer established Switzerlands's first chocolate factory in 1819.
- Two centuries later, the Cailler chocolate brand remained the oldest chocolate brand in Switzerland.
- 3 developments gave Switzerland a competitive advantage:
    - 1826 - Philippe Suchard invented the first machine that could mix cacao powder and sugar
    - 1875 - Daniel Peter (Cailer's son-in-law) invented milk chocolate; he had discovered that using powdered milk, rather than fresh milk, could prevent the development of mildew.
    - 1879 - Rodolphe Lindt created a machine that created melted chocolate, making it easier to pour and shape into moulds.
- 1908 - Theodor Tobler and his cousin Emil Baumann established Toblerone in Bern (Switzerland)
- They created a unique recipe (consisting of milk chocolate, almonds, honey, and nougat) and triangular shape and
- 1919 - up to 75 per cent of the chocolate that Lindt & Sprüngli produced was exported overseas.
- After industrialization pushed Swiss chocolate producers to rely heavily on overseas exports, global disruptions from the Great Depression and World Wars severely restricted their access to raw materials and international markets.
- In response, the industry strategically pivoted to secure and solidify its presence in the neighboring European markets of Germany, France, and Italy.

#### Made in Switzerland
- To be certified as "Swiss made," chocolate must undergo manufacturing in Switzerland from at least the conching stage, even though raw ingredients like cacao and sugar can be imported. 
- A key challenge for the industry is managing the rising costs of these imported materials, such as sugar, caused by newly imposed border protections.

#### Consumer Demand
- The Swiss chocolate market and Swissconsumers demanded:
    - Quality
    - Transparency
    - Sustainable Practices
- They wanted to know how the cacao beans were produced and how farmers were being treated along the supply chain.
- Switzerland was not part of the European Union (EU).
- it was a member of the European Free Trade Assocation (EFTA).
- Swiss food laws largely aligned with those established by the EU.
- Imported cacao had to meet various requirements, from food safety to environmental certifications, to attract Swiss buyers.
- Switzerland strictly rejects imported cacao that contains contaminants, including pesticides, heavy metals like cadmium, and microbial contaminants such as salmonella.
- Swiss chocolate importers and retailers typically adhere to ISO 9000 quality standards, utilize food safety management systems, and secure GlobalG.A.P. certification to ensure safe agricultural practices.
- Industry players commonly pursue recognized sustainability certifications, including Rainforest Alliance and UTZ Certified.
- To stand out in a high-standard market, companies seek niche certifications such as Fairtrade (which guarantees a living wage and prohibits child labor) and organic (which bans GMOs and strictly limits artificial fertilizers, pesticides, and antibiotics).
- Established in 2018, the Swiss Platform for Sustainable Cocoa aims for at least 80 percent of Switzerland's imported cocoa products to come from sustainable production by 2025.
- Members of the Swiss Platform for Sustainable Cocoa include major manufacturers like Lindt and Läderach, alongside retailers such as Coop and Aldi Suisse.
- Chocosuisse operates as an association of Swiss chocolate manufacturers responsible for outlining the industry's trade policies, quality standards, and sustainability efforts.
- Global manufacturers such as Lindt, Callebaut, Nestlé, and Mondelez supplement national standards by committing to their own proprietary sustainability strategies and programs.
- New companies entering the Swiss chocolate market must strive to meet these comprehensive quality and sustainability standards to successfully attract consumers and remain competitive.

#### Consumer Segments
- Chocolate purchasing decisions are driven by personal preferences, product attributes, sociodemographic factors, and economic considerations, with varying emphasis on price versus brand and quality. A 2020 study revealed that buyers fall into two main groups—those treating themselves and those purchasing gifts—and this purchasing occasion directly determines their price sensitivity.
- Subjective taste is the primary factor in buying chocolate, as consumers seek indulgent gratification and comfort rather than health benefits. Although taste strongly outweighs healthiness in purchasing decisions, consumers do associate "healthy" chocolate with higher quality. Because consumers are becoming more health-conscious overall, brands that successfully combine good taste with nutritional benefits can gain a competitive edge.
- Research indicates that Fairtrade labels positively enhance a chocolate's brand image. Furthermore, consumers are willing to pay a higher premium for Fairtrade-labeled products compared to those carrying other sustainability labels
- Customer loyalty is built primarily on previous consumption experiences regarding a brand's perceived quality and taste. When a consumer is unfamiliar with a brand, the appearance of the packaging becomes the key deciding factor. Given the indulgent and gift-giving nature of chocolate, both packaging and portion size significantly influence final buying decisions.
