## Executive Summary

This paper analyzes a fundamental paradigm shift in the European Union's economic governance. Driven by escalating geopolitical rivalry, supply chain disruptions, and the weaponization of energy, the EU is abandoning its traditional "regulatory state" model—which relied purely on market liberalization and competition enforcement. Instead, it is adopting a "market-shaping" model centered on economic security. This new approach actively coordinates investments, supports domestic manufacturing capacities, and selectively protects strategic industries to internalize systemic risks and build strategic resilience.

## Core Mechanisms & Findings

* **From "Market-Making" to "Market-Shaping":** The EU historically focused on removing trade barriers and enforcing competition rules (negative integration). It is now acting as a "networked developmental state," actively directing capital allocation and subsidizing strategic sectors to mitigate macroeconomic shocks.

* **The Four Pillars of Economic Security:** The EU's strategic pivot is operationalized through four distinct domains:
  1. **Industrial Capacity:** Using joint investments (IPCEI) and flexible state aid to build domestic production ecosystems.
  2. **Technological Sovereignty:** Viewing tech not just as an industry, but as a "general-purpose" bottleneck. Subsidizing this sector prevents innovation constraints across the wider economy.
  3. **Energy & Raw Material Resilience:** Diversifying supply chains to reduce the cost volatility and systemic vulnerabilities caused by concentrated dependencies (e.g., Russian gas, Chinese rare earths).
  4. **Trade & Investment Protection:** Using FDI screening and trade-defense mechanisms for "selective fortification"—managing risk without fully retreating from global integration.

* **The Efficiency vs. Resilience Trade-off:** The model of hyper-globalization optimized for static cost-efficiency. The new economic security paradigm explicitly accepts higher short-term costs (redundant capacities, localized production) acting as an "insurance premium" for long-term dynamic resilience.

## Sectoral Case Studies

The paper highlights how this structural transformation is actively reshaping specific EU sectors:

* **Semiconductors:** Prior to 2020, the EU held less than 10% of global semiconductor manufacturing. Through the *EU Chips Act* and *IPCEI Microelectronics*, the state is actively intervening to build local capacity and prevent downstream bottlenecks in the automotive and defense sectors.

* **Critical Raw Materials:** Facing a >90% import dependency on Chinese rare earths, the EU introduced the *Critical Raw Materials Act* to mandate domestic processing and supply diversification, turning raw material policy into core industrial strategy.

* **Energy:** Moving from a ~60% overall energy import dependency (and critical reliance on Russian gas), the *REPowerEU* initiative functions as a massive, state-directed macroeconomic stabilization effort to ensure predictable input costs for European firms.

## Policy Implications & Risks

While this shift is necessary for EU sovereignty, it introduces severe structural tensions. The relaxation of state aid rules risks fragmenting the Single Market, as fiscally stronger member states can disproportionately subsidize their own industries. Furthermore, the explicit selection of "strategic" sectors increases the risk of regulatory capture and the funding of "white elephants"—massive, inefficient public projects with limited long-term economic viability. The ultimate success of the EU's geoeconomic turn depends on balancing protective resilience with the competitive benefits of an open market.
