## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how the full historical trajectory of monetary policy rates—not just isolated recent changes—affects financial stability. Analyzing macro-historical data from 17 advanced economies over 150 years, alongside administrative loan-level data from Spain's 2008 crisis, the authors identify a specific monetary pattern that consistently precedes systemic banking crises: a **U-shaped rate path**.

## Core Mechanisms & Findings

* **The U-Shape Signature:** Rate hikes alone do not typically cause banking crises. A crisis is significantly more likely only if rate hikes are immediately preceded by a prolonged period of rate cuts (typically 5 years of cuts followed by 3 years of tightening).
* **The "Red Zone" Vulnerability:** Prolonged rate cuts systematically drive economies into a financial "red zone"—a period characterized by simultaneous, aggressive growth in both credit volumes and asset prices. 
* **Supply-Side Risk-Taking:** The data proves this boom is driven by credit *supply* rather than just demand. During the low-rate period, bank equity values artificially inflate, leading ex-ante weaker banks (those with higher non-performing loans) to aggressively expand cheap credit to ex-ante riskier borrowers (such as the real estate and construction sectors).
* **The Tightening Trigger:** When central banks eventually hike rates to combat inflation, it punctures the vulnerability built up during the cut phase. Rate hikes in the "red zone" cause sharp asset price reversals, massive spikes in loan defaults, and subsequent bank equity crashes.
* **Crises vs. Normal Recessions:** Standard, non-financial recessions are triggered directly by rate hikes regardless of prior policy. The U-shaped prerequisite is uniquely tied to *banking crises*, highlighting that financial instability requires both the build-up of bad debt and the trigger of tighter capital[cite: 187, 188, 194, 209, 214, 222, 225, 230].

## Micro-Level Evidence: Spain (1995–2008)
Spain's pre-2008 trajectory serves as the perfect empirical case study because its monetary policy (set by the ECB for core countries like Germany) was exogenous to its local booming economy. The Spanish Credit Register (CIR) loan-level data confirms that during the prolonged rate-cut phase, risky lending exploded. When rates finally rose, defaults heavily concentrated exactly where the supply-side vulnerability was highest: loans issued by weak banks to real estate developers.

## Policy Implications
If central banks keep rates low for extended periods, they build massive financial vulnerabilities. If they must eventually hike rates while the economy is in a "red zone," doing so aggressively is highly likely to trigger a systemic crisis. The authors suggest that in these scenarios, central banks should moderate rate hikes and instead rely on macroprudential tools and tighter banking supervision to cool the market.
